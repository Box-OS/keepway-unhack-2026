# UNHack 2026: Keeping the only road open (3–5 minute pitch)

**Lead with Graph 2** (`Graph2_Construction_Lifecycle_Costs.png`). It shows the whole argument in one picture: targeted hybrids cost 10–100× less than new roads or diversion schemes. Use Graph 1 next to show *why* the Kashechewan airport road floods and how high it must go.

**Strongest prototype community: Potlotek First Nation (Chapel Island Road at Matle'n's Brook).** It scores highest in the decision matrix (76/100, vs English River 73, Minegoziibe 60, Kashechewan 53): one site, central cost about **$1.8M**, one construction season, and first benefit by about **late 2028**. **English River (Hwy 918)** is the best-evidenced runner-up and the natural scale-up: 5 washout sites are Verified in Saskatchewan Hansard, at a central cost of about $9.9M.

## 1. Problem (30 s)
Four First Nations lose their only road access to floods, each through a different mechanism:
- **Kashechewan (ON):** the Albany River **ice jam** flooded the airport access road at 07:13 on 1 May 2025, and helicopters were needed (Reported). There is no all-season road, and evacuations come almost every spring at about $9–22M each (Reported, figures conflict).
- **English River / Patuanak (SK):** Hwy 918 washed out in **five places** in May 2026 (Verified, Hansard 7 May 2026). Closures lasted about 6 days, and burn debris blocked culverts. It also washed out in 2020.
- **Minegoziibe (MB):** a July 2026 rain flood closed Hwy 20 and caused a state of emergency ("landlocked in place", Reported). The failure sites are **not verified**.
- **Potlotek (NS):** Sept 2026 rainfall damaged the Chapel Island Road embankment at Matle'n's Brook. An engineering assessment said the culverts "may not handle the flow" (Reported). Water and wastewater mains run through the site.

## 2. What we tested (45 s)
For each community we tested Solution A (elevated / alternative road) and Solution B (diversion + storage) with numbers, then built a hybrid:
- **B fails at Kashechewan.** Diverting just 20% of the record Albany peak (2,000 of 10,400 m³/s, Verified upstream gauge) needs a channel about **214 m wide × 6 m deep** on a 0.2 m/km lowland slope, plus **864 million m³** of storage. That is about **430 km² of diked basin** in peatland. Even then it would not work, because the flood stage is set by the **ice jam downstream** (backwater), not by upstream flow. Rejected.
- **B is impractical elsewhere.** Diked ponds in muskeg (ER), a 155 ha reservoir for one stream (Minegoziibe), or a 5.6 ha pond (Potlotek) all cost **2–4× the hybrid**, add dam-safety and fisheries risk, and *still need the road culverts replaced*.
- **A as a new corridor** (333 km regional road, a new 87 km alignment, a 12 km bypass) costs $57M–$2.2B and crosses the same drainage. **A as a local raise** is what works.

## 4. Why it fits the geography
- **Hudson Bay Lowlands** (Kashechewan): the land is flat (about 0.5 m/km), over 90% peatland, and there is no high ground for storage. Height is the only defence against ice-jam backwater. The airport sits at 11 m, so the raised road ties into ground that is already high.
- **Boreal Shield / burn area** (English River): the failures are local crossings overwhelmed by snowmelt and debris, so the fix goes at those crossings, not along 87 km of road.
- **Parkland / Duck Mountain escarpment** (Minegoziibe): the floods come from flashy east-flowing streams. Crossing capacity is the lever, but only once the failure sites are known.
- **Cape Breton** (Potlotek): a small, steep brook under intense Atlantic storm rainfall. One properly sized crossing solves it with the smallest footprint near the Mniku (Chapel Island) National Historic Site.

## 5. Expected reliability gain [ILLUSTRATIVE]
Expected closure days per year fall by about **80–93%**: Kashechewan 2.0 → 0.2, English River 3.0 → 0.3, Minegoziibe 1.5 → 0.3, Potlotek 1.5 → 0.1. These are planning assumptions based on the reported closure history, not a return-period analysis. The real target is to keep each road passable through the 1:100-year event plus a climate allowance.

## 6. Implementation time (most likely, from early 2027)
Potlotek **≈22 months** (construction about 3 months, 1 season) · Minegoziibe **≈34 months** · English River **≈35 months** (2 seasons) · Kashechewan **≈70 months** (3 seasons, winter-road and barge logistics). Approvals and funding, not construction, are the critical path.

## 7. Maintenance
Post-breakup and post-storm inspection, culvert and debris clearing (about $1.5–6k per crossing per year), gravel upkeep, armour top-up (about 1% per year), regravel or resurface at 12–15 years, and culvert relining or armour renewal at 24–25 years. The Kashechewan stage camera also gives early warning.

## 8. Community and environmental benefits
Reliable access for medical evacuation, fuel, food and school; fewer costly evacuations; local construction jobs; open-bottom arches and larger culverts restore **fish passage**; works stay inside the existing road footprint (smallest wetland and cultural-site disturbance); and Potlotek's water and wastewater mains are protected.

## 9. Limitations (we say this up front)
- These are **Class 5 / order-of-magnitude** estimates (−50% / +100%). There has been no survey, no geotechnical work and no hydraulic model.
- **No measured flood elevations exist at any road.** The Kashechewan road crown (8.5 m) and 2025 stage (9.3 m) are **illustrative**. Nearest gauges are 100+ km away on an assumed datum.
- Unit rates are Alberta 2026 bid averages scaled by regional factors (Kashechewan 1.5–2.5×). Land, relocation and taxes are not priced.
- The Kashechewan road raise does not solve community flooding or relocation. It protects the evacuation and supply link only, and must be coordinated with the relocation decision.
- Minegoziibe's failure sites and every catchment area are assumed.
